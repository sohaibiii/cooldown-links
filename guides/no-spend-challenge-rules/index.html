No spend challenge: rules that last past the first week

A no spend challenge is easy to describe: for a set time, you buy only what you need. A lot of them fade out somewhere in the second week, and often for the same reason. The rules were never written down.

By the On Ice team · Updated 28 November 2026 · 6 min read

Choose a length you will finish

The well known versions are a no spend weekend, a no spend week, a no spend month (January is the popular one) and a no buy year. They are the same idea at different sizes.

If you have never done one, start with a week. A week you finish teaches you more than a month you drop on day nine, and you can go straight into a second one. The longer versions work best as a low buy, which we come to below.

Write three lists before day one

This is the whole method. It takes ten minutes, and it has to happen before you start, while you are calm and nothing is in your basket.

  1. Always fine. Rent or mortgage, bills, groceries, transport, medicine, and replacing something you rely on if it breaks. Nobody's challenge should include skipping a prescription.
  2. Not this time. The things you are pausing. Be specific to you: clothes, gadgets, home things, takeaway coffee, delivery food, in app purchases. A short honest list beats a long heroic one.
  3. Decided in advance. A birthday present for a named person, a trip already booked, one dinner out. Write them down with a rough cost.

The third list is the one people leave out, and it is the one that saves the challenge. An exception you chose in advance is part of the plan. An exception invented at the checkout is how the plan ends.

Decide where the wants go

You will still want things. A no spend month doesn't switch wanting off. It postpones buying. So the wants need somewhere to wait.

Keep one list for the whole challenge: the item and a rough price, each time something catches your eye. Don't use a shop's basket or wish list for this, because the shop will remind you about it all month.

The list does two jobs. During the challenge, writing a thing down is usually enough to let you close the tab. At the end, it tells you what the challenge was worth.

Make your phone match the rules

A rule in your head is up against a phone that is asking you to buy things all day. Even the odds.

  • Turn off shop notifications. Settings, Notifications, each shopping app, Allow Notifications off.
  • Unsubscribe as the emails arrive. One at a time, for the first week. Sale emails are hard on any challenge, and January has a lot of them.
  • Move shopping apps off your first Home Screen, or put a limit on them. The steps are in how to block Amazon on iPhone and work for any shop.
  • Block the websites you open from habit. See how to block shopping websites on iPhone.

The low buy version

All or nothing doesn't suit everyone, and it rarely suits a whole year. A low buy keeps the three lists and adds one allowance: a small fixed amount, or a set number of unplanned purchases, per month. Three, say. When they are used, they are used.

It sounds like a loophole. In practice it makes each purchase a choice between this thing and the next thing, which is most of what the challenge is trying to teach.

When you buy something anyway

It will probably happen once. It is one purchase. It is not the end of the challenge, and it says nothing about you.

Write down what it was and what time of day, then carry on the next morning as if the streak had never been the point. It wasn't. The point is to see your own habits clearly for a few weeks, and the purchase you didn't plan is the most useful thing you will see.

On the last day

  1. Read the wants list. Cross off everything you no longer care about. That is usually a good part of it.
  2. Add up the prices you crossed off. That number is an estimate of what the challenge kept in your account. It is the only way to see money you didn't spend.
  3. Buy what is left, if you still want it. Things that survived a month on a list are things you want. Enjoy them.
  4. Keep one rule. Not the whole challenge. The one change that was easy and made a difference, such as no shop notifications, or a night's wait before anything unplanned. The 24 hour rule is the usual choice.

Doing it with On Ice

We make On Ice, so here is how it fits, and where it doesn't. It has no challenge mode and no streaks, and we won't pretend otherwise. What it does is supply the two things a challenge most often lacks: a prompt at the moment you open a shop, and a wants list you don't have to remember to keep.

You choose your shopping apps and websites. Opening one shows a shield first. Tap Note it, give the thing a name and a rough price, and it goes on your list. A day later the app asks whether you still want it. Each thing you let go is added to your estimated savings, so on the last day the number from step 2 is already there.

For the hard evenings there are timed blocks, which close your chosen shops for 30 minutes or until a time you set. With Premium, daily limits and strict mode make the rules firmer.

If you still want something tomorrow and it is on your "decided in advance" list, the shop opens and you buy it. That is the challenge working, not failing.
